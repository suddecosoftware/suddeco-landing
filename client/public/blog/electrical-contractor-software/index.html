Electrical Contractor Software: A UK Buyer's Guide

Suddeco Team24 September 20268 min read

Electrical contractor software is usually bought for one of two reasons: to price work faster, or to keep the paperwork that a modern electrical business cannot operate without. Most firms need both, and most software does one of them considerably better than the other.

This guide sets out what an electrical contractor actually needs from software, how electrical pricing differs from general building pricing, and how to choose without paying for a platform built for a different trade.

What Electrical Contractor Software Must Cover

The commercial side comes first. You need to price from drawings quickly, hold a library of rates for the work you repeat, produce a quote a client can understand, and price variations without a negotiation each time. Electrical work is variation-heavy, so the variation process matters more here than in most trades.

The technical side comes second: a record of circuits, test results and certification for each job, produced as you go rather than reconstructed afterwards. Where that record lives — in the estimating platform, in a separate certification tool, or in a folder of paper — is a real decision, and pretending one tool will do everything perfectly is how firms end up with three systems and no single source of truth.

The third need is handover. When you finish a job, the client, the main contractor and the next engineer all need the same information: what was installed, what was tested, what is spare, and what is documented. Software that cannot produce that package cleanly creates work at the least profitable point in the project.

Pricing Electrical Work: Circuits and Points

Electrical estimating tends to be organised around points, circuits and distribution rather than around the elemental areas used for general building work. A quote might be built from counts of sockets, switches, light fittings and data outlets, priced either per point or per circuit, with distribution boards and containment priced separately.

That structure suits the trade, but it interacts awkwardly with client-side documents. A main contractor or cost consultant may ask for your price in an elemental or trade-bill format, while your own measurement is organised by points. Being able to present the same underlying price both ways saves hours of re-keying per tender.

Labour rates for electrical work need particular care. First fix, second fix and testing are different activities with different productivity, and a single blended rate hides the difference. Build your rates so that the split is visible, and you will find it much easier to explain a price or defend a variation.

Containment, cable and accessories are where material pricing drifts fastest. Copper prices move, and a rate that has not been reviewed for six months may be quietly wrong. Date your rates and review the volatile ones quarterly.

Estimating From Drawings in an Electrical Context

Electrical drawings are dense and often issued late in the design process. Small power layouts, lighting layouts and schematics each carry different information, and the coordination between them is frequently incomplete at tender stage.

Automated takeoff helps most with repetitive counting: light fittings, sockets, switches, data outlets and fire alarm devices repeated across many rooms. It helps least where the drawing omits installation detail, which is common in electrical packages. Treat automated counts as a rigorous starting point and add your own judgement about containment routes, cable lengths and the practicalities of installation.

The same principle applies as in general takeoff: if a tool cannot show you where a number came from and let you correct it in seconds, it will not survive contact with a live tender. Our guide to how takeoffs work covers the review discipline in detail.

Certification, Testing and Records

Electrical work is unusual in that the evidence of compliance is a document produced at the end. Certificates, test results, schedules of circuits and, where relevant, notification to a competent person scheme all have to exist and be accurate.

The practical question for software selection is how much of that you want inside the estimating platform. Some firms keep estimating and certification entirely separate, which is perfectly workable as long as both are digital. Others want the job record to carry through from priced scope to completed certificate.

Whichever you choose, resistance to re-typing the same job details three times is the test. If your software cannot carry a client name, address and circuit schedule from the estimate into the handover pack, you are paying for that duplication in admin time on every job.

Keep the current edition of the wiring regulations — BS 7671 — and your scheme's requirements in view when choosing. The tool has to support your compliance obligations; it does not discharge them.

Variations and Daywork

Variations are where electrical contractors make or lose money. The work is often genuinely additional, requested on site, and difficult to price retrospectively from memory.

The practical protection is a fast, consistent variation process: a simple form, a rate basis agreed in advance, and a signature or written confirmation before the work proceeds. Software helps by making the variation quick to produce — a short document referencing the original quote, the additional work and the price — rather than a long negotiation conducted by text message.

Agree daywork rates at tender stage, not at the point of dispute. A rate that is written into your terms and never argued about is worth more than a slightly higher rate negotiated under pressure.

Trade Tools or a General Estimating Platform

Trade-specific tools are quick to learn because the rate structure and item descriptions already match your work. They are usually narrow, and many are designed around certification or job management rather than around winning tenders.

A general estimating platform is broader: it measures from drawings, structures quantities, prices from your library and produces a professional output, and it will handle the occasional non-electrical work that most electrical firms end up doing. It asks more of you at setup, because you build your own rate library rather than inheriting one.

The deciding question is what percentage of your work is core electrical contracting. Above about eighty per cent, a trade tool's built-in structure is a genuine advantage. Below that, a general platform that you configure properly will usually serve you better and will not need replacing when you take on a different kind of job.

The Work After the Win

Estimating software wins the job; something else has to deliver it. The two systems are usually separate, and the seam between them is where information gets lost.

The practical minimum is that a won estimate should become a job record without re-keying: client, address, scope, price, and the variation basis you agreed. From there, a simple job sheet listing the circuits to install, the accessories to order and the test results to record is enough for most domestic and small commercial work.

Order lists matter more than they look. A priced estimate already contains quantities of fittings, accessories and containment, and turning that into a purchase list at the right moment is the difference between a job that runs smoothly and one that stalls waiting for a delivery.

Programme visibility matters too. Electrical work is frequently the trade that holds up completion, and being able to show a main contractor which circuits are complete, tested and certified is worth real money in repeat work. Even a simple per-area status list, updated weekly, changes how you are treated on site.
